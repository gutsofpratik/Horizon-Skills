# Price-to-Win Method

Use this reference when a PTW review needs a more rigorous pricing approach than a quick market check.

## PTW definition

Price-to-win is the estimated price or price range at which the government is most likely to buy a compliant solution from a credible bidder, considering the buying office, evaluation method, competitive field, incumbent behavior, scope, labor or product economics, and historical buying patterns.

Do not reduce PTW to "be the cheapest." In tradeoff procurements, a premium may be defendable if the technical and risk advantages are visible and aligned to the evaluation criteria. In LPTA or highly commoditized work, pricing discipline may matter more than differentiation.

## Two complementary views

### Top-down market view

Use historical and external market signals:

- predecessor award value and obligations
- same office/same scope awards
- task-order history under the same IDIQ/GWAC/BPA/schedule
- incumbent spend and option exercise patterns
- Service Contract Inventory footprint, reported hours, FTEs, derived hourly-rate context, and prime/subcontractor workshare for service-heavy contracts
- agency budget and acquisition forecast signals
- likely competitor awards and visible rates
- contract vehicle ceiling/rate constraints

This view answers: "What has the buyer paid and what have credible competitors accepted?"

### Bottom-up execution view

Use the likely technical and staffing solution:

- required CLINs or deliverables
- labor categories, hours, FTEs, shifts, surge requirements, and geography
- materials, travel, ODCs, software, cloud, licenses, equipment, or pass-through costs
- subcontractor/teaming assumptions
- indirect costs, fee, escalation, and risk reserve
- price realism, reasonableness, and balance constraints

This view answers: "Can the target company perform profitably at or near the target price?"

A strong PTW brief reconciles both views. If the market view and execution view conflict, show the gap and suggest technical, staffing, teaming, or bid/no-bid actions.

## Comparable selection rules

Prefer comparables that match more of these factors:

1. Same contracting office or program office
2. Same predecessor/follow-on relationship
3. Same contract vehicle or ordering channel
4. Same NAICS and PSC
5. Similar scope keywords and deliverables
6. Similar contract type and pricing basis
7. Similar set-aside or unrestricted competition
8. Similar place of performance and labor market
9. Similar period of performance and option structure
10. Similar size, complexity, clearance, or facility requirements

Use a three-tier comparable framework:

- Tier 1: Direct predecessor, same program, same office, or same vehicle with matching scope
- Tier 2: Same agency/office and materially similar scope
- Tier 3: Broader market analogs used only when direct evidence is sparse

Never mix ceilings, obligations, and total evaluated prices without labeling them. IDIQ ceiling values often overstate expected spend. Obligations can understate total value early in performance.

## Normalization patterns

Use whichever normalizations the data supports:

- Annualized value = total value / years of performance
- Monthly run rate = obligations / months elapsed
- FTE proxy = annual labor value / blended fully burdened rate
- SCI FTE/hour intensity = reported SCI hours, FTEs, subcontractor count, and subcontractor-hour share by fiscal year, vendor, award, IDV, agency, PSC, or NAICS
- Hourly rate comparison = fully burdened labor category rate by year/location/vehicle
- Unit price = total price / units, seats, devices, endpoints, transactions, claims, miles, square feet, or other demand driver
- Option-year trend = base-year price with escalation pattern
- Scope-adjusted value = comparable value adjusted down/up for missing/additional work areas

When using estimates, state the formula and assumptions in plain language.

## Scenario construction

Create three scenarios unless the user asked for only a quick check.

### Aggressive / low scenario

Use when the customer needs a price-led win or LPTA posture. This usually assumes lean staffing, lower fee, optimized labor mix, stronger subcontractor discounts, or a willingness to accept margin pressure. Flag execution risk.

### Competitive midpoint scenario

Use as the default target if the target company has no special premium differentiator and needs a credible, executable bid. This should be supported by multiple comparables or a blend of top-down and bottom-up evidence.

### Premium / value-based scenario

Use when the target company has strong technical discriminators, incumbent advantage, reduced transition risk, special vehicle position, or documented performance advantages. Tie the premium to evaluation factors. Do not recommend premium pricing if the solicitation is price-dominant and technical superiority is unlikely to overcome the delta.

## Confidence scoring

Use these labels:

- High: Direct predecessor or strong same-office comparables, clear evaluation criteria, visible price basis, and labor/unit signals.
- Medium: Several good comparables and clear scope, but some price basis or labor mix assumptions remain.
- Low: Sparse comparables, early-stage notice, unclear evaluation method, or missing pricing attachments.
- Very Low: No direct comparables and mostly broad analogs, budget hints, or customer assumptions.

Always include what would raise confidence.

## Labor-rate analysis

For service procurements:

1. Extract labor categories from the RFP, pricing sheet, PWS, SOW, or incumbent/task-order records.
2. Map categories to comparable GovTribe/GSA/vehicle rates where available.
3. Use Service Contract Inventory where available to size reported hours, FTEs, incumbent footprint, prime/sub workshare, and derived hourly-rate context.
4. Group by seniority, clearance, labor location, remote/on-site requirement, and year.
5. Compare rate bands rather than overfitting to exact titles.
6. Estimate blended fully burdened rates only when the mix is available or reasonably inferable.
7. Separate direct labor, fully burdened labor, subcontractor rates, fee, and ODCs when known.

Flag likely rate issues:

- rates far above comparable vehicle rates
- unrealistically low senior labor categories
- inverted junior/senior rates
- unexplained escalation
- unbalanced option-year pricing
- mismatch between technical staffing promises and pricing volume
- SCI-reported FTEs, hours, or subcontractor workshare that conflict with the proposed staffing narrative

## Competitor posture heuristics

Use these as hypotheses, not facts:

- Incumbents may tolerate lower transition cost but may carry legacy staffing or higher wrap.
- New entrants may price aggressively to displace an incumbent.
- Large primes may price higher but lean on risk reduction and past performance.
- Small businesses may compete on lower overhead but can be constrained by staffing depth.
- Vehicle holders with existing customer footprint may price more confidently than vendors without agency history.

Label these as inferred pricing posture unless directly supported by award/rate data.

## Deliverable levels

### Quick PTW check

Use when the user asks a simple question or provides little context:

- recommended range or posture
- confidence
- 3 evidence points
- data gaps
- next action

### Standard PTW brief

Use the full structure in SKILL.md.

### Deep PTW analysis

Use when the opportunity is high-value, the requesting company provides proposed pricing, or the request needs consultant-level depth:

- detailed comparable universe
- competitor matrix
- normalized price/rate model
- scenario assumptions
- sensitivity analysis
- capture action plan
- price-volume compliance checklist
